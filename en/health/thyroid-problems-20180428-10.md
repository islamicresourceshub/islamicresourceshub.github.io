The thyroid gland is the organ that regulates the body's rhythm. This gland is involved in numerous physiological functions, including metabolic processes. It is located in the neck and is approximately two inches in size, shaped like a butterfly with its wings spread.

The thyroid gland primarily produces two hormones: triiodothyronine, known as T3, and thyroxine, known as T4. These are produced when the thyroid gland is stimulated by the thyroid-stimulating hormone, or TSH, which comes from the pituitary gland. The T4 hormone is converted into the more active T3 hormone in the body's peripheral tissues.

The primary functions of the thyroid hormone are as follows: it increases the body's oxygen consumption, thereby generating more energy; it helps maintain body temperature; it increases heart rate; it accelerates nerve impulses; it plays a very important role in the intellectual development of children and during pregnancy; it lowers cholesterol levels in the blood; it increases the absorption of carbohydrates; it aids in milk production during the breastfeeding period; and it helps maintain a regular menstrual cycle and reproductive health in women.

**More Common in Women**

Thyroid diseases are most commonly seen in women. Thyroid problems can arise at any stage of life, starting from pregnancy. However, studies suggest that approximately one in five women is likely to experience thyroid problems at some point in their lives.

**Thyroid Disorders**

There are many types of thyroid diseases. Sometimes, the gland may not be able to produce hormones in the correct manner, or it may produce them in excess. In some individuals, hormones may be produced, but they do not convert into the active T3 form. Alternatively, even if other functions occur correctly, the body's immune system may fail to coordinate them properly.

The main thyroid problems are hypothyroidism, or underactive thyroid, and hyperthyroidism, or overactive thyroid. In the current era, the primary reason for the increase in thyroid diseases is the imbalance of various nutritional factors and an incorrect lifestyle. Iodine is the most essential element for the functioning of the thyroid gland. In addition to this, a deficiency in trace elements such as zinc and selenium can also lead to thyroid diseases. Radioactive iodine treatment, autoimmune disorders, and genetic factors are also causes of the disease. Some recent studies indicate that many chemicals added to food and artificial flavor enhancers also cause such disorders.

Hypothyroidism is the most common of the thyroid problems. Its main symptoms include fatigue, lethargy, unexplained weight gain, dry skin, hair loss, inability to tolerate cold, constipation, depression, excessive bleeding during menstruation, increased blood cholesterol levels, and swelling in the face and limbs. The main symptoms of hyperthyroidism are unexplained weight loss, excessive sweating, rapid heartbeat, anxiety, irritability, fatigue, hand tremors, diarrhea, and insomnia.
