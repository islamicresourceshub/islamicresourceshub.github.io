Against Public Nuisance

മുസാഫിര്‍ · 2018-05-19 · Opinion & Analysis

Public Nuisance: Legal Definition

The Indian Penal Code defines public nuisance as an act or omission that causes any common injury, danger, or annoyance to the public. Poisoning water used by the public for drinking or bathing constitutes a public nuisance. Polluting the air with toxic substances is also a public nuisance. Similarly, discharging sewage onto public pathways or roads is considered a nuisance. When an individual or institution is legally bound to perform a duty but fails to do so, it is termed an omission. Maintaining urban cleanliness is a primary legal duty of a municipality.

The Ratlam Municipality Case

A situation of this nature occurred in the Ratlam Municipality of Madhya Pradesh. Due to incomplete drainage work along the roads, sewage overflowed and stagnated in various places. Poor residents living in huts were forced to use the roadsides for defecation due to the lack of public toilets. Waste from homes and hotels was also dumped on the streets. The municipality failed to take any action to prevent this dumping or to clear the waste in a timely manner.

Legal Actions Against Public Nuisance

The Code of Criminal Procedure contains a section (133) that empowers the Sub-Divisional Magistrate to take action against public nuisance. Upon receiving a complaint, the magistrate can summon the opposing party, examine evidence, and if the complaint is found to be true, order the party to remove the nuisance. The Indian Penal Code includes a provision to take action if such an order is disobeyed. This is the essence of the provisions regarding public nuisance under the Code of Criminal Procedure.

Supreme Court Verdict

The magistrate was convinced that the complaint against the Ratlam Municipality was true. He ordered the municipality to clean the drains, complete the drainage work, and construct public toilets for the poor. The municipality's primary defense was a lack of funds. To avoid punitive action, the municipality challenged the magistrate's order in the Sessions Court. The case eventually reached the High Court and finally the Supreme Court. By then, seven years had passed! In the judgment, Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer noted that the primary duty of a municipality is urban sanitation and that a municipality failing in this duty does not deserve its name. The court made the state government a party to the case and directed it to provide the necessary financial assistance to the municipality. A warning was also issued that punitive action would be taken if the nuisance was not removed within the specified time.
