A Bit of History

According to human rights organizations, their total population is between 1.5 and 2 million. The indigenous among them have been living in the Rakhine region of Burma (Myanmar) since 1400 AD. Their spoken language is Rohingya.

Bengal Sultans and Burmese kings shared borders, and the Bengal inhabitants brought to this region for agricultural work and other tasks during that era became part of this community. After the British gained control of Burma, the East India Company brought many Bengal inhabitants to the fertile Arakan region for farming. In the early 19th century, many also migrated to the Arakan (Rakhine) region from Chittagong in Bengal for work. When Burma gained independence in 1948, some who had migrated to Burma were also among them. There are about 135 ethnic groups in Myanmar like this. Groups like the Chin, Kaman, and Kamei are migrants from various countries.

The Beginning of Problems

For centuries, Buddhists and Rohingya Muslims living together in the Arakan region maintained friendship and interaction. Living on both sides of the Naf River (the border between Burma and Bangladesh), they maintained trade and social relations.

In 1942, the **Burmese Independence Army**, formed under the leadership of General Ne Win, began to cultivate anti-Rohingya sentiment among Buddhist believers. They succeeded in fostering the belief that Rohingya Muslims were laborers of the East India Company and that their growing population was a threat to their own survival.

During the Second World War, the Japanese army invaded Burma. While Japan, with its large Buddhist population, and local Buddhist militias tried to gain dominance in Burma, the Rohingyas, with British support, blocked their advance in the northern Arakan region. However, in other areas, minorities such as Chinese migrants, Christians, and Rohingya Muslims became widespread targets of nationalist attacks. Many of them fled to Bengal.

When Pakistan was declared an independent nation in 1947, a section of the Rohingyas wished to join that country. However, Muhammad Ali Jinnah refused to interfere in the internal affairs of Burma. A large section also wished to remain in Burma. In 1948, Burma was declared an independent country, and initially, it embodied unity in diversity, encompassing about 135 different ethnic and religious groups.

But the situation changed when General Ne Win seized power from the civilian government in 1962. To win over the Buddhist majority in the country, he used military support to slaughter minority Rohingyas, abuse women, and drive them out of their homeland. A Buddhist terrorist organization operating under the name Arakanese Independent Movement attacked the Rohingyas extensively. The problems between them continued.
