![](/assets/uapa-wielding-sedition-law-20170114-9/14Jan17-02.jpg)Pointing to a bullock cart struggling along a muddy path, a child asked his father: "Father, why are you only whipping the bull on the left? Both are walking at the same pace, aren't they?"

The father replied: "It's not because that bull is lazy, my son; it's because the driver holds the whip in his left hand."

It was the communist theorist Karl Marx who observed that the state is an instrument of oppression. Kautilya, in his advice to Chandragupta Maurya, suggested that the state could maintain its authority by using royal power to kill people, frame others for crimes, and throw them into dungeons. Current events are a testament to the fact that many regimes, in both theory and practice, are now driven by such Machiavellian principles.

This is precisely why, since the inception of the bureaucracy, every official has been taught that their duty is to implement the law according to the whims of the ruling masters. The terms "NDA's army" and "LDF's police" have found a place not just in the media, but in the public consciousness, precisely because of this perspective.

I am speaking about the UAPA. This draconian law is descending like a thunderbolt upon the heads of citizens who merely wish to see goodness prevail in the country. It is a fact that many ruling parties are governed by the realization that there is no better legal tool than the UAPA to trap and capture anyone they dislike. The shocking reality presented by recent news is that, unlike in the past, they are now using it without a shred of mercy.

![](/assets/uapa-wielding-sedition-law-20170114-9/14Jan17-03.jpg)Section 113 of the pro-British Penal Code, drafted by Lord Macaulay during the colonial era, is the ancestor of the Indian Penal Code's laws regarding sedition. Between 1863 and 1870, the British administration realized that existing laws were insufficient to withstand the strong resistance of the Wahhabi movements. Consequently, they re-introduced Section 124, which had previously been omitted, and enacted stringent legislation. Beyond those involved in the Wahhabi movement, many famous figures, including *Bangobasi* editor Jogendra Chandra Bose and Bal Gangadhar Tilak, were portrayed as traitors under this law.

Numerous communal riots that occurred after the freedom struggle and the partition prompted national diplomats to retain this section. Although some amendments were made in 1951, the law remained as it was. Following this, the UAPA was initiated through a recommendation by a committee appointed by the National Integration Council in 1963 to protect the country's sovereignty and maintain administrative stability. The draft of this law, the *Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967*, was introduced in the Indian Parliament that same year, passed by both houses, and came into effect on December 30, 1967, after receiving the President's assent.

Although it faced significant criticism for violating many fundamental rights of citizens in the name of national security, the UAPA was made more complex through amendments in 1969, 1972, 1986, 2004, 2008, and 2012.

It was after the 2008 amendment that the UAPA began to be used as readily as other laws. The trend of using the law to suppress dissenters—similar to AFSPA (Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, 1958), MCOCA (Maharashtra Control of Organised Crime Act, 1999), POTA (The Prevention of Terrorism Act, 2002), and TADA (Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act)—increased significantly after this period. The Mumbai terror attacks on November 26, 2008, served as a major catalyst. Against this backdrop, on December 16 of the same year, stringent changes were made to the law without sufficient discussion or study. Since anti-terror sentiment was running high, the then-Home Minister P. Chidambaram pushed these amendments through with the support of the Left parties.

Many provisions of the UAPA are drafted in a way that allows for arbitrary interpretation, enabling anyone to be branded a criminal. This law grants the power to question, seize property, conduct raids, and arrest any citizen based on a complaint from an individual or the mere suspicion of an official. The greatest danger lies in the fact that any police officer can exercise this power, which should ideally only be possible with a court order or judicial warrant. In the wake of widespread protests against the invocation of UAPA against Rajeesh Kollankandi, a clerk at the West Hill Polytechnic College in Kozhikode, for holding an unauthorized demonstration; against Kamal C. Chavara for allegedly insulting the national anthem; and against Nadeer for allegedly providing shelter to Maoists, it has become clear that Loknath Behera's statement—that such actions should not be taken without instructions from above—lacks legal backing.

It is abundantly clear that by changing the phrase "reasonably suspected" in the Code of Criminal Procedure (CRPC) to "credible reason," the interests of investigating officers are prioritized. Furthermore, if corporations, government agencies, organizations, or even individuals file a complaint based on the instructions of a Superintendent of Police, it becomes mandatory for any citizen to produce all their information. The legal action taken against the world-renowned Islamic scholar Zakir Naik, his organization, and his channel is clear evidence that this law can be used to investigate anyone, no matter how high-ranking, and trap them without hesitation. The seeds of this witch-hunt were sown by Zakir Naik's speeches found on the Facebook page of one of the Dhaka blast suspects. The only reason he became "undesirable" to Indians, to the point where he could not even visit his own father's funeral, is that the law is in the hands of the left-handed bullock cart driver holding the whip.

Under Section 43D(2) of the UAPA, the initial detention period for a person taken into custody is 6 months, i.e., 180 days. After 90 days, the prosecutor only needs to convince the court that there is progress in the case to continue the custody. Moreover, the burden of proof on the prosecutor to prove the innocence of the accused does not apply in UAPA cases. Instead, the accused must prove their own innocence. Under 43D(5), there is no possibility of bail unless the public prosecutor allows it. Additionally, under Section 43(A), authorities have the power to attach or freeze the assets and sources of income of those suspected of participating in terrorist activities. It is due to this legal obstinacy that Dawood Uwaid, Zahir Sayed, and Sameed Ahmed Shaikh, the leaders of the Mumbai-based Buruj Realization, are still facing trial today in connection with textbooks in Peace Schools.

Violations of citizens' rights under the UAPA do not end here. This law provides for the arrest of individuals and the banning of organizations based on limited evidence. A citizen can be kept in police custody for 30 days and in detention for 180 days without sufficient evidence; a case can be filed against someone for possession of items even if there is no intent to commit a crime; if an arrested individual's organizational connection is proven, the organization they belong to can be banned without significant explanation; property can be seized without a warrant; the right to assemble guaranteed by the Constitution can be diluted to open the gates of sedition; and people can be thrown behind bars through the misuse of print and electronic media... the UAPA is rich with many such laws that stand in the way of a citizen's peaceful life.

Under TADA, from 1984 onwards, 18,686 people were arrested in Gujarat, 15,314 in Punjab, 15,225 in Jammu and Kashmir, and 12,715 in Assam. Excluding the Sikhs in Punjab, the majority of the accused were Muslims. In 1987, under the leadership of K.P.S. Gill, about 7,600 people were arrested under the sedition law to suppress the Sikh minority. Only 15% of them were put on trial, and only 2% were convicted.

When the TADA law was repealed, the statistics of the total cases filed were shocking to anyone who believes in humanity. Out of 75,500 people charged, only 15,991 were convicted. The remaining sixty thousand were innocent! That is to say, all these people were considered criminals in the eyes of society for all this time!

In Chhattisgarh and West Bengal, the government, with the support of monopolies, used this law to suppress all protests by the poor against the seizure of their agricultural land by mining companies. Since these poorest of the poor cannot afford legal aid, they have to languish in jails indefinitely.

The experience of Irshad Ali, who was released last month after spending 11 long years in jail under the UAPA only to be found innocent, is harrowing. Serving as an informant for the government, his job was allegedly to go into Muslim pockets, attract unemployed Muslim youth to extremism, and then betray them to be arrested during terrorist activities. Irshad Ali revealed that there are even "farmhouses" in the country operating with state support to nurture terrorists, and that he was framed and arrested under the UAPA when he tried to back out, disgusted by such clandestine activities. Although he gained media attention years ago by writing an open letter to the Prime Minister from Tihar Jail, it took eleven long years for Irshad Ali to prove his innocence.

While the predecessors of the UAPA, TADA and POTA, danced a demonic dance over the rights of citizens across the country, Kerala was a model for the nation through mature political intervention. But when the UAPA came, the situation changed. Similar news started coming from Kerala. IT professional Yahya Kammukutty from Mukkam was released after seven long years in jail, found to be innocent. There were four other Malayalis besides Yahya in this case. Even after admitting that he filed the complaint due to pressure from the Mumbai police—and that it wasn't even in the context of him being a terrorist—the complainant's revelation was ignored, and the scholar from Kannur is still in jail under the sedition law for months. Moreover, many, from Abdul Nasser Madani to Zakariya, are still facing endless trials. Meanwhile, teachers who alleged that the national anthem is a hymn to the British, "Gokris" who ranted about the "Muslim pig-load" in Malappuram, and Sanatan leaders who take pride in the demolition of the Babri Masjid all roam free in the country without any issues. When this happens, one cannot blame a common man for suspecting that in Kerala, laws like the UAPA are meant only for Muslims, just like prayer or Hajj.

Even if there were no significant cases or arrests in Kerala in the early days of the UAPA, the fact that the numbers in recent years have been many times higher than in previous years is clear evidence that political parties have begun to use this law for vendetta politics. Media reports indicate that more cases have been registered under the UAPA this year than in all previous years combined. Although official figures for 2016 are not available, media information suggests this.

18 people in the Hubli conspiracy case, Mufti Abdul Qayyum who was released after 13 years in the Akshardham blast case after being found innocent, Abdul Kaleem who was accused in the Malegaon blast case and later proven innocent through Swami Aseemanand's confession... there are many such people. There are many other helpless people still languishing in dungeons without their innocence being proven. Above all, there are many innocent victims of the Varanasi blasts, the Millennium Park shooting, the Batla House encounter, and the Bhopal encounter killings, who had to end their lives through a bullet or an explosion without understanding what their crime was, and before they could understand the verdict of the state legal system and the public conscience.

 the whips of state tyranny can come looking for us too. There is no particular reason needed for it. It could be voice clips taken out of context from a speech you gave years ago. It could be the interpretive possibilities in fragments of conversation you set for children learning to read. It could be the drops of blood that ooze from the edges when you tear out pages of past history to reenact memories of sacrifice. It could be natural flaws in your look or speech that appear at the wrong time. Anything, everything, can lead you to a charge of sedition, and through that, to an endless prison term or a bullet that rushes in at the wrong time and leaves you motionless. Therefore, keep your eyes open; hold hands together. Yes, we too must live.
