## Arriving in a Wheelchair

Inside, amidst the sighs of anxious women hearing the occasional cries from the second stage of labor, and the new mothers lying with pale faces and white identification bands on their wrists, she arrived in a wheelchair. She looked to be about sixty-five. Beneath the wrinkles on her face and the intense pain indicated by her hands pressed firmly against her abdomen, a mischievous glint remained in her eyes, waiting to break through. As she was slowly helped onto the bed, neatly covered with the blue sheets of the **labor room**, she let out faint, indistinct sounds of pain.

## To the Question of What Happened

To the question of what was wrong, she began to reply in a murmuring voice that was difficult to understand:

> "I had an operation on my lower abdomen a few days ago. For two days, I have had severe stomach pain and vomiting. It hurts even when I touch the top of my stomach."

Upon asking for more details, we suspected a pelvic abscess—a collection of pus inside. We sent her for an ultrasound scan, which showed some fluid buildup, but nothing was definitive. We could not decide whether to perform a digital vaginal examination or attempt to drain it with a needle. The surgeon examined her rectum, but found nothing significant. Finally, it was decided to perform a contrast CT scan.

## A Skin Test

When I spoke to her **daughter-in-law** waiting outside, the response was not very favorable. Finances were the main issue. It was only after realizing the gravity of the situation that her daughter, who arrived later, gave her consent. Then came the next hurdle! A skin test revealed that she was allergic to the dye that needed to be mixed with water before the scan. The scan could only be performed if she was given medication starting 13 hours prior. In short, it could only happen the next morning.

## Affectionate Despite the Pain

She lay there among many young women—some about to give birth, some who had just delivered, others coming to check on their baby's movements, or seeking relief from the discomforts of pregnancy. Despite her pain, she smiled affectionately at everyone she saw. Beyond the usual rush, she did not hide her dissatisfaction at having to be looked after, yet she carefully warned the nurse coming to administer medicine, saying, "My dear, I am allergic to many medicines; it is written in the file." Observing everything around her with great precision, she had memorized the schedule of the doctors and nurses. She did not forget to call out to those trying to sneak away for a cup of tea or a moment of sleep, asking loudly, "Are you leaving, dear? When will you be back?" I also heard her comforting a patient crying in pain: "I have also given birth to two, my dear—a daughter and a son. There will be some minor pain, don't worry about it." Since she was vomiting everything she ate, she was on a drip. Even though there was no improvement in her illness or treatment, I felt a pang of worry as the brightness on the grandmother's face seemed to increase.

## The Second Injection Before the Dawn CT Scan

While receiving the second injection before the dawn CT scan, she said in a voice lower than usual:

> "I pray that my illness does not get better quickly. I am content just lying here. If I were at home, I would only have to listen to my daughter-in-law's scolding. Do you know what I had to hear just for saying I had a stomach ache? She said, 'Don't just make things up, old woman. It is enough that you say it; we are the ones who have to take you to the hospital and spend money. Just lie there quietly.' I was sent from here with instructions to bathe in warm water after the operation. But if I ever asked for warm water, she would give me cold water and walk away saying, 'Bathe in that if you want.' When the pain didn't subside after two days, I couldn't bear it anymore and called my daughter. She has children in the fourth and eighth grades. She left everything behind to bring me here. Can she stay here day and night? That is why the daughter-in-law is standing there."

Fortunately, no one bothered to explain that the daughter-in-law had actually gone home the previous day.

She said all this in a suppressed, fearful voice. She added at the end, "Doctor, please don't tell her anything; that would be enough."

## Not Knowing How to Console Her

Not knowing how to console her, the nurse stood there holding the needle. The doctor standing nearby held the grandmother's wrist. I made up my mind to speak to the daughter-in-law. Wiping away the moisture that had begun to gather in her eyes so no one would see, she took control of the situation again:

> "Don't worry about those things, children. Take the injection; don't we have to do the scan tomorrow?"

The words of comfort scattered into the air...

## The Scan the Next Morning

We performed the scan the next morning. The hardened pus was removed. She was moved to the post-operative ward. The wrinkled sheets were replaced with new ones. Amidst the noise of the **fetal monitor** loudly announcing the heartbeat of new life, everyone forgot her sorrows. In the ward above, while worrying about the day she would have to get better and go home, the grandmother lay there, hiding a lifetime of light in her eyes, offering only smiles to everyone.
