Manusmriti and the Caste and Varna System

ഉസ്മാൻ പാലക്കാഴി · 2017-02-18 · Islamic History

Endless Caste and Varna Discrimination: 2

The Purusha Sukta, found in the tenth mandala of the Rigveda—a text highly revered by Hindus—states that the Brahmana was born from the mouth of the Virat Purusha, the Kshatriya from his arms, the Vaishya from his thighs, and the Shudra from his feet! The Gita also accepts this classification. Consider verses 87 to 91 of the first chapter of the Manusmriti, which is said to embody human dharma without regard to time or place:

“For the protection of this world, he assigned specific duties to those born from the hands, thighs, and feet of the Virat Purusha, who possessed brilliant radiance. He ordained that Brahmanas have the right to study and teach the Vedas. He appointed them to perform sacrifices and accept gifts for the welfare of others” (88).

“The duty of the Kshatriya is the protection of the people. Performing sacrifices, studying the Vedas, and abstaining from indulgence in the senses—these are the duties prescribed for the Kshatriya” (89).

“Vaishyas have been appointed to raise cattle, give gifts, study the Vedas, conduct trade, lend money, and engage in agriculture” (90).

“God has ordained that the Shudra’s only duty is to serve those mentioned in the other three varnas with obedience” (91).

“Even if a Brahmana is wicked, he is worthy of worship. A Shudra, however, is not worthy of worship even if he has conquered his senses” (Parashara Smriti 8:33).

This makes it clear that individual character traits are irrelevant; the Brahmana is superior simply because he was born into the highest varna. Let Manu speak:

“Whether he has studied the Vedas or not, he possesses divinity. Even if he engages in work suited for lower varnas, a Brahmana remains a Brahmana. Because he is born in the highest varna, he is like a god. His instructions are inviolable commands. Everyone must obey him” (Manusmriti 9:317).

Manu’s Jurisprudence

The Manusmriti states that the severity of a crime should be determined by one’s varna. If a Brahmana insults a Shudra, he only needs to pay a fine of twelve panas. But if a Shudra insults a Brahmana? The Shudra’s tongue must be cut out! It is clear that Manu’s jurisprudence maintains a regressive nature that fails to see even the Shudras, who stand at the lowest rung of the varna structure, as human beings. Was it not according to this jurisprudence that Brahminical hegemony oppressed the marginalized in such a vile manner? Dr. Vellayani Arjunan writes:

“...In society, the divisive thinking of 'savarnas' (upper castes) and 'avarnas' (lower castes) grew daily like parallel lines that could never meet. The marginalized communities were always victims of various forms of exploitation and oppression. Once the Nambuthiris, the Keralite transformation of the Aryans, became the masters of society, they attempted to cement various forms of dominance over the non-Brahmin natives. In the name of religion, Brahmin priests became spiritual guides. The authority to control temples, worship, and other divine affairs became the monopoly of these Brahmin priests. This situation of dominance paved the way for the feudal system to grow strong in Kerala and for an invincible varna hegemony to flourish in a way that suppressed all other communities. Temples, rituals, modes of worship, and other tantric-mantric divine rites became the monopoly of the priestly class. People belonging to the 'avarna' category were denied all life amenities. They were forbidden from walking on public roads, building good houses, wearing decent clothes, wearing ornaments made of metals like gold or silver, conducting weddings in beautiful pandals, serving feasts to relatives, or even entering temples or their premises. The upper castes created terrible untouchability systems that forced the lower castes to stay far, far away from their masters... The upper castes even had the authority to brutally beat or kill those 'avarnas' or marginalized people who violated the untouchability laws by not keeping the prescribed distance. This monstrous situation continued without the slightest change until about the beginning of the 19th century. With the Temple Entry Proclamation issued by the Maharaja of Travancore, Sree Chithira Thirunal Balarama Varma, on November 12, 1936, all marginalized communities legally gained the freedom of worship. Yet, the demon of caste did not completely vanish from Kerala society; it remained like a persistent affliction. During that painful period when humans were driven away by other humans in the name of caste and treated as lowly outcasts, when there was no freedom of movement, education, worship, social liberty, or even the right for women to cover their breasts or wear good clothes, and when a few upper-caste people and the priestly class that kept them in check turned Kerala into a 'lunatic asylum', Sree Narayana Guru rose like the sun of human liberty in the darkened Kerala with messages of liberation” (Vijnanakairali (Journal of the Kerala Bhasha Institute), pages 15, 16, July 2006).

Brahminical Hegemony and Scriptures

Who can say that the aforementioned actions of the Brahminical elite have no backing from Hindu scriptures? Are those who ignore these realities not, in truth, denying their own codes of dharma?

There are those who argue that varna is determined by karma (action) and not by birth, claiming that through merit and deeds, a Shudra can become a Brahmana and a Brahmana a Shudra. The reality is that caste and varna are fixed by birth into a specific caste or varna. None of their actions are considered in that regard. Do the scriptures not say that if one born in a Shudra family performs the duties of a Brahmana, it is a violation of their 'swadharma' (own duty)? Is there any example of a Shudra or Vaishya becoming a Brahmana through merit and deeds? Is there any individual who received such a promotion alive today?

Can 'avarnas' or non-Brahmin 'savarnas' become priests? The Brahmin stance that they should under no circumstances be temple priests is indeed backed by Hindu scriptures. The Brahmin-savarna stance that denies temple priesthood to 'avarnas' is not, in one sense, the responsibility of the savarna people or their organizations; rather, it is the responsibility of the Hindu scriptures themselves. Fundamental Hindu scriptures do not support the view that Brahminhood can be attained through karma or that karma, not birth, is the basis of Brahminhood. The idea that Brahminhood can be attained through karma, and that 'avarnas' and lower castes can attain Brahminhood through 'shodashakriya' (sixteen rites), was promoted by later Hindu reformers, Hindutva politicians, and those pursuing the communal goals of Hindu empowerment, based on the concept of 'broad Hindutva'.

Political Goals and Hindutva

How does an 'avarna' become a Hindu? There is a political goal behind the fact that movements like the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh in modern India have come forward to promote this view, which lacks the backing of the scriptures that define a Hindu or the foundational documents of Hindutva; it is not based on a genuine cultural perspective on Hindutva. Because many, like Ambedkar, were able to recognize the goals of those who introduced the philosophy of turning 'avarnas' into Hindus, they raised the argument that only 'savarnas' can be Hindus. It was after studying Hindu dharma deeply that Ambedkar opined that 'avarnas' are not Hindus.

Reformers of Hindu dharma were at the forefront of adopting this new theory of dharma determination, which lacks scriptural support. While some movements like the Arya Samaj acted as agencies distributing Brahminical Hindutva to everyone, they also attempted to synthesize religious reform with Aryanism. It was people like Dayananda Saraswati, who defined 'Aryan' as noble or Brahmana, who gave massive publicity to the argument that anyone can become an Aryan through 'shuddhi' (purification) rituals. The goal of the Aryanists was to subject 'avarnas' to a very mysterious 'savarna-ization' and the deeply hidden political goals behind it. It is with this intention that Guruji Golwalkar criticizes casteism and untouchability in Bunch of Thoughts (Vichardhara). He cites a resolution passed at the Vishwa Hindu Sammelan held in Udupi in 1969. See it:

“With the goal that the entire Hindu community should be unified in a spirit of unbroken unity and that it should not be fragmented by tendencies and feelings of touchability and untouchability, Hindus everywhere in the world should follow the principle of unity and equality in their mutual friendships” (Vichardhara, pages 405, 406).

Every line of Vichardhara makes it clear that such calls for unity are for the mere political goal of forming a 'Hindu Rashtra' and not for the betterment of society or the Hindu religion itself. The truth is that the command to eliminate all distances is actually contrary to many commands in the epics, smritis, and Vedas.

“In the flow of pure moral devotion, places where one can come together in a common spirit of brotherhood, submerging differences like touchable and untouchable, such as bhajans, kirtans, festivals, and recitations of Ramayana-Mahabharata stories, can be arranged for this purpose” (Vichardhara, page 410).

It is a sight to behold today that things like observing 'Ramayana Month' are being popularized to bring all castes together. The success of the Sangh Parivar lies in the fact that common people do not understand that the goal behind all this is not the upliftment of Hindu dharma, but a purely political one.

“The priestly Hindu religion maintains a reluctance to see Dalits and other marginalized sections as equals, except in matters of voting. The post-independence generation has acquired 'American equality' instead of 'Upanishadic equality'! The proof of this was the self-immolation that took place in Delhi during the Mandal agitation. It was the immense sense of loss at having to give up some of the benefits they were enjoying to the marginalized that prompted them to take this extreme step. The reluctance to see the marginalized as brothers and lift them up indicates how powerfully the influence of the 'dharmashastras' continues through generations” (Hinduvinte Pusthakam, Shashidharan Chembazhanthi, page 121).

This quote reveals how superficial and deceptive the Hindu unity upheld by Vichardhara and others is. What kind of unity is created by bringing the Panan, Parayan, and Pulayan together in one place and arranging bhajans, kirtans, festivals, and Ramayana-Mahabharata recitations? What benefit do those poor people get from that? Were the Sangh Parivar workers who carry Vichardhara around not the ones who fought fiercely against the implementation of the Mandal Commission report to provide job reservations for such people? If giving a few benefits to the marginalized was so disagreeable to them that it prompted their workers to even commit self-immolation, what is the goal of the Hindu unity they promote?

Yes, on one hand, the 'savarnas' nurture casteism. On the other hand, they cultivate Hindu sentiment among the 'avarnas' only for political gain and take them along. One does not see this group ready to lift a finger against caste discrimination. If they had, there would have been a reduction in the news of inhuman acts against 'avarnas' that frequently emerge from North Indian states.
